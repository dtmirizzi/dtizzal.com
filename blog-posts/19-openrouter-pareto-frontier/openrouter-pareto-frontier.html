Stop Picking Models From the Leaderboard. Track the Pareto Frontier.

Every week someone asks me "what's the best model right now?" and every week the honest answer is "best at what, for how much?" A leaderboard answers half of that question. It sorts models by score and pretends price doesn't exist. But I don't buy intelligence in the abstract. I buy tokens, by the hundreds of millions, and every one of them has a price tag.[1]

So I built the thing I actually wanted: every model on OpenRouter that has a public benchmark score, plotted by score against price, with the Pareto frontier drawn through it. The frontier is the set of models you can't beat on both axes at once. If a model isn't on it, there's something else that's at least as smart and cheaper. Play with it, then I'll tell you why I think this line is the most important chart in applied AI right now.

What "dominated" means, and why it's brutal

A model is dominated when some other model scores at least as well and costs no more. That's it. No weighting, no vibes, no "but it's good at X." It's the weakest possible claim you can make about one option being better than another, which is exactly why it's useful: if a model is dominated on the benchmark you care about, there is no price sensitivity under which picking it is correct.

On the Intelligence index, 46 models have scores. Eight are on the frontier. The other 38 are dominated by something.

Here's the part that surprised me. The single most-used model in the window, deepseek-v4.1-flash-20260910 at roughly 127 trillion tokens, is dominated. Its predecessor deepseek-v4-flash-20260731 scores ~10 points higher on the Intelligence index and is a hair cheaper. The second most-used model, glm-5.3-flash, sits right on the frontier — but zoom out and only about 24% of scored tokens are going to frontier models on Intelligence.[2]

Some of that is legitimate. Benchmarks aren't your workload, latency matters, a newer snapshot might be better at the one thing you need. But a lot of it is inertia. Someone picked a model three months ago, wired it into a config file, and nobody's looked since. That's the thing tracking the frontier fixes.

The frontier moves faster than your config file

Look at the release dates on today's Intelligence frontier. Of the eight models on it, six shipped in the last eight weeks: GLM 5.3 Flash (Aug 26), Muse Spark 1.3 (Sep 2), Claude Opus 5.5 (Sep 21), MiMo v2.6 Pro (Sep 21), Claude Sonnet 5.5 (Sep 28), Claude Haiku 5.5 (Oct 7). The only survivors from before August are a free Gemma 4 and the July DeepSeek V4 Flash.

That's a half-life measured in weeks. If you made a careful, rigorous, defensible model choice in July, there's a good chance it's dominated today. Not wrong at the time — just stale. The choice didn't get worse; the world got better around it.

This is why I think of the frontier as something you track, not something you look up. A leaderboard snapshot tells you who's winning. A frontier tracked over time tells you when your decision expired.

The knee is where the money is

Going from GLM Flash to Opus 5.5 is about 52× the price for 16 more points. Most of those points are bought in a single jump to Sonnet 5.5; the last step to Opus doubles your bill for 1.6 points. Coding is starker: Gemini 3.8 Flash hits 76.3 at $1.13/M, and the top of the frontier (Claude Fable 5.1) hits 81.6 at $15/M — about 13× the price for 5 points.

That doesn't mean you shouldn't pay for the top. For a hard agentic task where failure costs a human an hour, five points can be worth 13× easily. For classifying support tickets, it's lighting money on fire. The frontier makes that tradeoff visible instead of implicit, and it tells you where the knees are — the spots where one step buys a lot of score and the next buys almost none. Those knees are where your default model should probably live, with escalation to the next step only when a task needs it.

Which frontier depends on what you're measuring

Flip the benchmark tab and watch the line reshape. A few models are robust across all three — glm-5.3-flash and the July deepseek-v4-flash sit on the Intelligence, Coding, and Agentic frontiers, which is a remarkable thing for two models that cost around a dime per million tokens. Others are specialists: Gemini 3.8 Flash is dominated on Intelligence and Agentic but is a frontier model on Coding.

The lesson is that "best value" is meaningless without naming the axis. If your workload is mostly tool calls, the Agentic frontier is the one you should be watching. If it's code generation, Coding. And honestly, the best version of this chart uses your eval on the Y axis — public benchmarks are just the cheapest proxy available to everyone.

Constraints have a price, and the frontier shows it

The country and open-weights filters aren't just for curiosity. Lots of teams have real policy constraints: no models from certain jurisdictions, open weights only so you can self-host, whatever your security team decided. Every constraint you add removes points from the plot and can only push the frontier down or right. The gap between the constrained and unconstrained frontier is what your policy costs you.

Sometimes that cost is near zero. Filter to US-only on Intelligence and the cheap end barely moves: Claude Haiku 5.5 at $0.15 (43.4) slides in where GLM 5.3 Flash at $0.12 (41.8) was. Sometimes it's a ceiling: filter to open weights only and the frontier tops out at Muse Spark 1.3 at 48.1 — nearly 10 points below the best closed model. Knowing which one you're in is the difference between "this policy is free, keep it" and "this policy costs us a tier of capability, let's talk about it on purpose."

How I'd actually use this

  1. Pick a default at the knee, not the top. Route the bulk of traffic to the cheapest frontier model that clears your quality bar on your own evals.
  2. Escalate up the frontier, never off it. When a task needs more, move to the next frontier step. Never escalate to a dominated model — by definition there's a cheaper option that's at least as good.
  3. Re-check weekly. When a new model lands on the frontier near your default, run your evals against it. This is the whole point of using a router like OpenRouter: switching is a string change.[3]
  4. Price your constraints. When someone proposes a model policy, show them the constrained frontier next to the unconstrained one.

Caveats, because there are always caveats

Price here is a naive blend — the average of input and output price per million tokens. Real workloads are lopsided: RAG and classification are input-heavy, generation is output-heavy, and the frontier can shift a lot depending on your ratio. Benchmarks are third-party aggregates, they lag new releases, and plenty of models with real usage have no score yet so they don't appear. Free tiers ($0) are rate-limited and often not something you can build a product on. And token volume tells you what's popular, not what's good.

None of that undermines the core idea. It just means the chart is a starting point for your own frontier, not a substitute for it.
